PhD Research: Hydrogen and Methane Aircraft Safety

Aircraft Design
Hydrogen Safety

Quantifying how hydrogen- and methane-specific leak-safety constraints affect long-haul aircraft performance and informing future certification frameworks.
Published

September 17, 2026

Overview

Liquid hydrogen (H\(_2\)) and methane (CH\(_4\)) are leading candidates for decarbonizing long-haul aviation. However, these fuels differ substantially from Jet A in their leak and ignition behavior, requiring systemic safety considerations from the earliest design phases.

This research connects three domains: certification frameworks, hazard physics, and aircraft performance. I couple leak-consequence solvers (such as HyRAM+) with multidisciplinary design optimization (MDO) to quantify how passive leak-mitigation requirements affect transport efficiency across fuels and tank architectures.

At a glance

TipHeadline results
  • Safety has a measurable cost, and it depends on how much leakage you design to tolerate. For a 7500 nmi, 400-passenger commercial transport mission (Boeing 777-300ER-class), designing for a 10 mg/s passive-threshold leak rate (PTLR) raises passenger-specific transport energy by roughly 2-5%. At 20 mg/s, the increase is roughly 3-11%.
  • Spatial separation drives the penalty, not ventilation. Keeping hot jets due to fuel-leak-supplied flames away from structure grows the aircraft, and is especially impactful if driving the wing geometry. Ventilation drag to mitigate leaked fuel buildup and subsequent explosion risk is a minor contributor.
  • Methane is penalized more than hydrogen at the same leak rate because its flames require larger offsets.

Full results are in Key Results; the framework and models behind them are in Methods.

Background

Jet A forms slowly evaporating pools with small ignitable regions. Leaked H\(_2\) and CH\(_4\) behave very differently: they vaporize quickly, mix readily with air, form larger ignitable regions, and ignite at much lower energies. As a result, tanks should stay at positive pressure with a gaseous-fuel ullage, which prevents ignitable boil-off/air mixtures from forming inside them.

Ground-based hydrogen systems offer a baseline set of leak-mitigation strategies. However, there is no established way to apply them to aircraft or to relate them to certification requirements. This work aims to fill that gap.

Key Contributions

  • Designated Leak Zone (DLZ) framework. A formal classification of leak zones for H\(_2\)- and CH\(_4\)-fueled aircraft, modeled on the 14 CFR § 25.1181 fire zones used for Jet A, to handle the unique vapor and ignition risks of alternative fuels.

  • Physics-based consequence modeling. Leak-consequence models that map the ventilation flow rates and spatial separations needed to passively mitigate jet fires, explosions, and cryogenic damage.

  • MDO integration. An aircraft design procedure that optimizes vehicle configuration under leak-safety constraints. It evaluates transport energy as a function of fuel, tank layout, and the maximum leak rate mitigated passively, termed the passive-threshold leak rate (PTLR).

  • Performance impact quantification. Flame-driven spatial separation (especially for wing geometries) dominates system-level penalties over ventilation requirements. At equal leak rates, CH\(_4\) incurs larger penalties because of its larger spatial offsets.

Methods

The sections below describe the leak-safety framework, the consequence models, and the MDO setup behind the results above.

Designated Leak Zones and the Proposed Leak Safety Framework

The framework rests on two constructs: designated leak zones (DLZs) and passive-threshold leak rates (PTLRs).

The hazard zone definitions are informed by FAR 25.863 (flammable fluid fire protection) and FAR 25.1181 (designated fire zones). Zones are defined by their proximity to fuel and to heat or ignition sources:

  • Fuel zone: the internal volume of fuel-containing components, such as tanks and pipes, where liquid, supercritical, or gaseous fuel is present during normal operation.

  • Designated leak zone (DLZ): any region where leaked fuel could enter or accumulate. This includes compartments containing fuel-system components and regions that fuel lines or fittings pass through, but excludes regions continuously open to the external atmosphere.

  • Designated fire zone: a region associated with equipment identified under FAR 25.1181, such as engine and auxiliary power unit compartments, where flammable fluids and high-temperature ignition sources may coexist. These zones must meet the applicable designated fire zone requirements.

  • Occupied areas: areas where occupants and cargo are present during normal operations. They may contain ignition sources associated with people, equipment, or cargo. Fuel shall not be present in occupied areas during normal operations or single-failure scenarios.

Two requirements follow from these definitions:

  • Adjacency. A single failure may not introduce flammable fluids into occupied areas. Occupied areas therefore cannot be adjacent to designated fire zones or fuel zones; a DLZ must sit between them as a leak-mitigation buffer.

  • Pressurization. This localizes single-failure leak consequences to the DLZ. Fuel zones are kept at positive pressure relative to the DLZ, so a loss of containment pushes fuel outward into the DLZ. Occupied areas are also kept at positive pressure relative to the DLZ, which biases leaked fuel away from the cabin. Managing leaks within the DLZ is then the primary means of preventing personnel exposure.

Passive-Threshold Leak Rate (PTLR)

Leak consequences, and the design needed to mitigate them, depend on leak rate. Passive mitigation is always available, but its design requirements scale with the leak rate it must accommodate, so a characteristic leak rate must be chosen. That rate, expressed as a fuel mass flow rate, is the passive-threshold leak rate (PTLR).

An aircraft’s PTLR is the maximum leak rate for which it is passively safe, meaning safe without leak detection or response.

Each DLZ is represented by a simplified duct-like segment, and parametric models estimate the consequences of a leak within it.

Leak source. Models disagree substantially for cryogenic leaks near saturation, with mass-flux differences up to about 8\(\times\).

Explosion mitigation. Ventilation limits the accumulated fuel mass, and thereby the overpressure from combustion.

Jet fire and heating. Spatial separation and ventilation determine how hot nearby structure gets.
